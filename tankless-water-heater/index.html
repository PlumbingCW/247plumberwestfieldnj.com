A tankless water heater in Westfield, NJ is an on-demand unit that heats water only while a tap is open, using a gas burner or electric elements and a heat exchanger instead of a stored tank. We install them, repair them when they throw error codes or run cold, and flush the scale out of them on a schedule. They are not right for every house, and we will say so.

The common questions have little to do with the brand. They are about flow, gas supply, venting, and whether the local water will scale up the heat exchanger. Those are the things the sections below take on.

Flow rate and temperature rise, in plain numbers

Two numbers decide whether a tankless unit keeps up. The first is flow, measured in gallons per minute: how much water the household wants at once. The second is temperature rise, the number of degrees the unit must add to the incoming water to reach the setting you want.

Incoming water is colder in winter, which raises the rise you need and lowers the flow the unit can deliver. A unit that handles two showers in July may struggle with them in January. We size to the cold-weather case, since that is when you notice a shortfall. Count the fixtures that run together, and tell us if you have a large soaking tub or body sprays.

Gas capacity is the question most buyers skip

A tank burner is small and runs for a long time. A tankless burner is large and runs only when you ask for hot water, so it draws far more gas at the moment of use. The gas piping and the meter that served a tank may not carry that load alongside a furnace, a stove, and a dryer.

Older houses are where this comes up most. About 37% of homes in ZIP 07090 were built in 1939 or earlier, and the median build year is about 1953, so the original gas piping may be small and may have been extended over the years.

We count appliances and measure the run before recommending a unit, and we will tell you if the line needs upgrading first. For the broader planning side, see how a new heater location gets planned.

Venting: what a tankless unit needs to breathe

Condensing units

  • Run cooler exhaust, so the vent can be plastic pipe
  • Need a drain for acidic condensate, often to a floor drain
  • Vent runs are easier to route through walls
  • Need the condensate path checked every year

Non-condensing units

  • Run hot exhaust, so they need stainless vent pipe
  • Do not produce condensate, so no drain is needed
  • Vent runs are more restricted by clearance rules
  • Cost less up front, use more fuel

Repair: what the usual tankless faults look like

When a tankless unit stops working, the display usually names the problem. Write down the code before you reset anything.

Cold water in the middle of a hot shower

Short bursts of cold are often caused by a clogged inlet screen, a low flow that does not trip the burner, or a mixing valve elsewhere in the house.

Error code and no hot water

Ignition failures, flame loss, and exhaust blockages each have their own code. The cause is a failed igniter, a dirty sensor, or a restricted vent.

Water too hot or swinging

A fouled heat exchanger or a dirty thermistor misreads temperature. Descaling often settles it.

Rumbling or kettle sounds

Scale on the heat exchanger traps tiny steam pockets. Left alone, the exchanger overheats and fails.

Condensate leak at the base

A blocked condensate trap or a cracked drain line drips onto the floor. It is a repair and not a reason to scrap the unit.

Tankless flush and descale: the service that keeps the warranty and the unit alive

Heating water drives dissolved minerals out of it, and they stick to the hottest surface: the inside of the heat exchanger. A tankless unit has narrow passages that scale restricts. The result is less flow, noisy operation, error codes, and eventually a cracked exchanger. A flush removes the scale before that point.

The standard method is to close the isolation valves and connect hoses to the service ports. A small pump circulates a descaling solution, usually a mild acid like white vinegar or a manufacturer-approved cleaner, through the heat exchanger for roughly 45 minutes to an hour. Then the unit is flushed with clean water, the inlet screen is cleaned, and the gas and vent connections are checked at the same visit.

A unit with no service valves cannot be flushed properly, so we add a valve kit when one is missing.

How often to flush depends on your water, and we cannot say a number without a reading: test your tap with a hardness kit, and ask New Jersey American Water for its latest water quality report. Hard water means yearly or more often. Soft water can stretch it. Some warranties ask for proof of regular maintenance, so check yours and keep the records. The annual visit also covers the exhaust and condensate checks that a descale alone would miss.

What affects the cost of a tankless job

We price the job after we see the gas line, the wall, and the vent path. A flush is a smaller visit and an install is a larger one, and the line between them depends on the house. Call the dispatch line and we will walk through the details for your unit.

  • Unit type and capacityCondensing, non-condensing, gas, and electric units differ in price, and so does the flow they deliver.
  • Gas pipe upgradeA larger branch from the meter or a new run is often the biggest hidden item.
  • Vent pathShort through-wall runs are simple. Long runs, roof penetrations, and flue capping add labor.
  • Isolation valves and condensate drainService valves make flushing possible. A drain for condensate may need a pump.
  • Scale condition for service callsA unit that was never flushed takes longer to descale than one on a schedule.
