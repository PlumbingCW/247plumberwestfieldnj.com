Repair a Leaking Toilet Tank in Westfield, NJ

Water around a toilet is not always a leak, and a silent leak inside the tank can run for weeks. Here is how to tell which one you have.

To repair a leaking toilet tank in Westfield, NJ, you first work out where the water is escaping: from the tank to the floor, from the tank into the bowl, or only as condensation on the outside. A toilet tank is the ceramic reservoir behind the bowl that holds a flush of water, and it leaks at its bolts, its supply connection, its flapper seat or through a crack.

Some of these are quick owner fixes and some mean the tank or toilet should be replaced. Start with the dye test below, because it separates a silent internal leak from a visible one in about 15 minutes, and then match what you find to the repair.

The Dye Test That Finds a Silent Leak

A running tank that leaks into the bowl rarely makes obvious noise. This test catches it.

  1. 1

    Dry the outside first

    Wipe the tank and base so that any new water you see afterward is real, not old drips.

  2. 2

    Add color to the tank

    Drop a few drops of food coloring or a dye tablet into the tank water. Do not flush.

  3. 3

    Wait and watch the bowl

    After 15 minutes, look at the bowl. Color showing up means the flapper or its seat is leaking into the bowl.

  4. 4

    Check the outside for beads of water

    Dry the tank again with a paper towel. If drops return only in humid weather and are colorless, it is condensation, not a leak.

  5. 5

    Check the base and bolts

    Put a dry paper towel under the tank bolts and at the supply connection. Colored water on the towel points to the exact joint.

The Water Bill Is the Other Clue

A toilet that leaks into the bowl can waste a surprising amount of water without any noise, and the first sign is often a bill that has crept up with no change in habits. Check your meter with every tap and appliance off. If the leak indicator or the dial keeps moving, the water is going somewhere, and the toilet is one of the first places to look.

If the dye test is clean and the meter still moves, the leak is elsewhere: a supply line, a hose bib, or a pipe in a wall or slab. That is when leak detection with listening gear and a moisture meter earns its keep.

What a Homeowner Can Safely Check

These checks need no tools beyond a screwdriver and a towel.

  • Lift the lid and look at the water level against the mark on the overflow tube or tank
  • Press the flapper down by hand. If the hiss stops, the flapper or seat is the problem
  • Look at the chain. Too tight holds the flapper open, too loose tangles it
  • Feel under the tank bolts and the supply joint with a dry paper towel
  • Rock the toilet gently. Movement means loose floor bolts, which need a pro to check the flange
  • Close the shutoff valve and see whether it holds, then reopen slowly

When to Stop and Call a Plumber

Swapping a flapper, adjusting a float or replacing a supply line are tasks many owners handle. Anything involving a cracked tank, a rocking toilet, a seized shutoff valve or water coming from the floor around the base needs a licensed plumber, since state law limits plumbing work to a master plumber, an authorized plumbing contractor, or their employees.
