What Is Safe for a Homeowner and What Is Not

Disposals injure people who assume the unit is off. This list separates the checks anyone can do from the work that should wait for us.

  • Safe: switch the wall switch off and unplug the unit before touching anything around it.
  • Safe: look into the hopper with a flashlight, and pull out visible debris with long tongs or pliers, never with fingers.
  • Safe: press the red reset button on the bottom of the unit after it has cooled for a few minutes.
  • Safe: use a hex key in the center of the bottom of the unit to rotate the shaft back and forth and free a jam.
  • Not safe: any hand inside the hopper, even with the power off.
  • Not safe: pouring drain cleaner or boiling grease in a unit that is already clogged.
  • Not safe: disconnecting the drain outlet while the sink holds water.
  • Call us: a leak from the body or the housing seam, repeated tripping of the breaker, or a smell of burning from the motor.

Jammed and Flooding the Sink: What to Do While You Wait

A disposal that will not run and will not drain can leave a sink full of dishwater. These are the steps that protect the kitchen.

Power off, plug out

Use the wall switch, then unplug the cord under the sink if there is one. A hardwired unit gets its breaker switched off.

Stop adding water

Skip the dishwasher, because it drains into the disposal. Hold off on sink use until the line is open.

Dip out standing water

Use a cup or ladle to lower the sink water so you can see the drain. Wear gloves, since the water carries food and grease.

Clear the cabinet

Move cleaners, bags and paper out from under the sink. If the unit is leaking, put a shallow pan under it and mark where the water is coming from.

How a Disposal Installation Goes, Start to Finish

Swapping a unit is a few hours of work when the old one comes out cleanly. This is the sequence we follow.

  1. 1

    Turn off power at the breaker and confirm it

    A disposal is wired or plugged in under the sink, so we cut power and test the circuit before loosening anything.

  2. 2

    Disconnect the dishwasher hose and drain

    Both sit on the disposal's side. We catch the water in the trap and check the hose for cracks.

  3. 3

    Remove the old unit from the mounting ring

    A twist on the lower ring releases the unit. We scrape off the old putty and inspect the sink flange.

  4. 4

    Mount the new unit

    The sink flange is sealed with new putty or gasket, locked into the mounting ring, and the unit is hung from it.

  5. 5

    Reconnect drain, dishwasher and power

    We set the discharge elbow at the right angle, knock out the dishwasher plug in the inlet if it was not done, and reconnect the electrical.

  6. 6

    Run water and test for leaks

    We fill the sink, run the disposal and check every joint with a dry paper towel before leaving.

Dishwasher Connections That Cause Mystery Leaks and Smells

The dishwasher drain hose connects to a nipple on the disposal. If the plug inside that nipple was never knocked out on a new unit, the dishwasher cannot drain. If the hose clamp is loose, water seeps at the connection and runs down the unit.

Dishwasher drain hoses are also routed in a high loop under the counter. A hose that sags allows dirty water to flow back into the dishwasher, causing a smell or a drain that stops partway through the cycle. We check the loop whenever we replace a disposal.

What Belongs in a Disposal and What Does Not

Fine in small amounts, with cold running water

  • Soft scraps like cooked vegetables and fruit peels
  • Small bits of soft food left on dishes
  • Ice cubes occasionally, which scrape the grinder walls
  • Citrus rinds in small pieces to freshen the unit

Keep out

  • Grease, oil and fat, which cool and coat the drain line
  • Coffee grounds, eggshell membranes and starchy rice or pasta in quantity
  • Bones, fruit pits and corn husks
  • Fibrous items such as celery strings, potato peels in bulk and artichoke leaves
  • Anything non-food, including utensils and bottle caps

What Affects the Price of Disposal Repair and Replacement

A jam is cheaper to clear than a leaking unit is to replace. You get a clear price before work starts. The factors below move the total.

  • Repair versus replacementFreeing a jam or reseating a gasket costs less than a new unit, and a cracked body cannot be repaired.
  • Unit size and motor typeA larger motor, a quieter model or a batch-feed design costs more to buy and sometimes needs a different mounting.
  • Electrical setupA plug and outlet under the sink is a simpler swap than a hardwired unit or a missing switch.
  • Drain conditionIf the trap, tailpiece or line behind the unit is clogged or corroded, that is added work.
  • Hour of the callNight or weekend work can cost more than a daytime visit for the same job.

Homeowners policies often cover sudden accidental discharge, such as a hose that bursts, but not wear, corrosion or slow seepage, so read the policy. If water is on the floor now, call (908) 357-1542 and tell us where the leak is.
