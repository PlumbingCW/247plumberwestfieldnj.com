While You Wait: First Moves When a Commercial Line Fails

Staff and tenants usually reach for a mop first. These four moves protect the building and the people in it, and they give us what we need on the first call.

Close the nearest shutoff

Try the fixture stop, then the tenant or riser valve, then the building main. A valve that is stuck or leaks at the stem is worth telling us about.

Clear the floor and cut power at risk

Move stock, paper and electronics off the floor. If water is near outlets, panels or a walk-in cooler compressor, leave the breaker alone and call the building electrician.

Stop using the affected fixtures

A backed-up kitchen line gets worse every time a dishwasher drains or a restroom flushes. Post a sign and keep staff off those sinks.

Photograph and note the source

A few pictures and a note on which floor, which fixture and when it started help your insurer and help us plan tools and parts.

After-Hours Failures We See in Restaurants, Shops, Offices and Apartment Buildings

The building type tells us a lot before we arrive. These are the failures that most often lead to a night or weekend call.

Restaurant kitchen backup

Grease and food solids pack the line between the prep sink and the trap or main. Floor drains start bubbling at close, when the dish machine runs hardest.

Retail restroom overflow

A high-use public restroom flushes paper towels, wipes and trash that a residential toilet would never see. The blockage sits past the fixture and floods the floor.

Office riser leak

Water stains on a ceiling tile usually mean a failed joint or supply above. The wet spot is rarely under the leak.

Multifamily stack backup

When a shared drain stack clogs, the lowest unit floods first. Several tenants may call at once, and the answer is in the shared line, not in any one apartment.

Water heater or boiler failure

A storefront with no hot water can fail a kitchen or salon day. Commercial tanks and mixing valves are sized differently from home units.

Planned Grease Trap Cleaning, Step by Step

Grease trap cleaning is scheduled work, not an emergency, though a skipped cleaning is how many kitchen emergencies begin.

  1. 1

    Locate and open the trap

    Under-sink units sit near the prep area. Larger in-ground interceptors have a cover that may be under equipment or a parking surface.

  2. 2

    Measure what is inside

    We look at how deep the grease cap and the settled solids are. A trap packed with solids needs cleaning more often than the calendar suggests.

  3. 3

    Pump or bail out the contents

    Water, grease and solids all come out, and the tank walls and baffles are scraped. Leaving the old layer behind defeats the cleaning.

  4. 4

    Check the baffles, lid seal and outlet

    A cracked baffle lets grease pass straight through. A bad lid seal lets odors into the kitchen.

  5. 5

    Set a cleaning interval and log it

    We suggest a schedule based on what we saw. Haulers, the water utility or the local authority may set their own records rules, so ask them what applies to you.

Backflow Testing: Keeping the Building's Water Out of the Public Supply

A backflow assembly sits on a line where dirty water could be pulled back into the public supply during a pressure drop. Restaurants, car washes, irrigation systems, boilers and some fire lines commonly have one. The assembly has check valves and relief parts that wear, so it is tested rather than assumed to work.

Testing involves isolating the device and reading pressure differences across its valves with a test gauge. If a part fails, the assembly is repaired or replaced and tested again. How often a test is required, and who receives the result, depends on your utility and local authority, so ask New Jersey American Water and the Westfield construction office before you book.

Floor Drains and Lift Pump Overflow

A floor drain is the last safety valve in a kitchen, a mechanical room or a basement. It fails in two ways: the trap dries out and lets sewer gas up, or the line behind it clogs and sends water back out. Pouring water into a drain that rarely gets used refills the trap, but a drain that backs up needs the line cleared.

A lift or ejector pump handles water from fixtures below the sewer level, such as a basement restroom. When the float sticks or the pump burns out, the pit fills and overflows onto the floor. Do not keep using the fixtures feeding that pit. Call us with the pit's condition and whether an alarm is sounding, and see our sump pump page for how related pumps work.

Commercial Leak Detection: What We Need to Know on the First Call

Commercial leak calls often end without a booking, and that is usually because the scope is large and access is hard, not because the leak is hidden. A meter that keeps turning in a building with six tenants, a slab, a roof unit and a fire line could be any of a dozen things. We would rather say so than send a crew with the wrong tools.

To help us decide, call the dispatch line and tell us the building type and age, what changed in the water bill or meter, which areas are wet, and who can give access to ceilings, mechanical rooms and the street valve. Tools include a pressure gauge, a moisture meter and a camera, but none of them shortens a job that needs walls opened. Our leak detection service explains the methods in more detail.

What Drives the Cost of Commercial Plumbing Work

We cannot give you a figure before we see the building, and we will not guess over the phone. You get a clear price before work starts. These are the things that move it.

  • Access and building layoutA basement cleanout is quick. A drain buried under a kitchen line, a locked mechanical room or a ceiling that must be opened adds time.
  • Size and type of the lineA 2 inch fixture line and a 4 inch building drain call for different equipment, crew size and time.
  • After-hours timingNight and weekend work can cost more than planned daytime service. A scheduled grease trap visit usually costs less than the same job after a kitchen has shut down.
  • Parts and code requirementsBackflow assemblies, grease interceptors and pumps vary by size and rating. Work must follow New Jersey's plumbing subcode, which adopts the 2021 National Standard Plumbing Code.
  • Permits and coordinationSome jobs need a permit and an inspection, and permit rules vary by job, so ask the Westfield construction office before work starts.

Business and property policies often cover sudden accidental water discharge but not wear, corrosion or slow seepage, so read your policy and talk to your agent about business interruption.
